This is a Philco 46-1226, a walnut console radio and record changer from just after World War II. It was my father's family's radio in Hopewell, Virginia, and it hasn't played in my lifetime. For decades its power cord was too far gone to even plug in.

Inside, though, it was remarkably intact: clean, complete, and apparently never modified. That made the goal simple to say and fussy to do. Get it working again, safely, while changing as little of the original as possible.

## The restoration

I'd warmed up on a Philco 40-130 first, so I went in with a plan: replace the parts that are known to fail with age, test everything else, and leave the original wiring layout alone.

**Capacitors.** Eighty-year-old paper and electrolytic capacitors are the usual suspects in an old radio, and they can take other parts with them when they fail. Over a few late-August sessions I replaced fifteen paper capacitors with modern film equivalents, keeping each one on its original connection points. Two more are buried under the tuning assembly, and I left them there. Getting to them risks more than it fixes.

**Power supply.** One of the big power resistors had failed open and another had drifted well out of spec. I replaced both, and rebuilt the filter capacitors on new terminal strips while leaving the original cans mounted so the chassis still looks right.

**Power and safety.** A flaky power switch came back with contact cleaner. Then came a new cord and modern safety-rated line capacitors. The first power-ups were done slowly, through a dim-bulb limiter and then a Variac, with a meter watching the high-voltage supply.

**The static.** Once it was powered, the radio had an intermittent static that wouldn't go away. Swapping tubes between sockets showed that the static followed one of the original 6K6 output tubes. A replacement RCA 6K6GT arrived on September 18.

**First sound.** On Saturday morning, September 19, the radio played a broadcast station for the first time in decades. I tested the audio with a small low-power AM transmitter feeding it music, and it sounded great. The two dial lamps were dead, too; new #51 bulbs had the glass lit again by September 21.

**Alignment.** The dial was reading badly low, as much as 70 kHz off in places. On September 26 I aligned the receiver, using WHPY on 1590 as the reference for the top of the dial. Afterward, stations landed where the glass said they should, within about 10 kHz.

**One last crackle.** A popping noise that was hard to place turned out to be the original 7AF7 converter tube. Pulling it silenced the noise, and tapping it with a chopstick made it pop on every tap. A new-old-stock replacement went in on October 8, and the radio has been quiet since.

The record changer is a separate project for another day. The shortwave band works but has only been spot-checked against a couple of stations, not fully aligned.

## By the numbers

- **Original price:** $193.85, or about $3,350 in today's dollars
- **Number made:** 40,621, a count that also includes the January 1947 Model 47-1227
- **Version:** Code 125. Philco used code numbers to mark changes made during a model's run, and Code 125 came after Codes 121 and 122.
- **Tubes:** 8
- **Bands:** AM broadcast, plus shortwave from 9.3 to 15.5 MHz

Philco went back to building home radios a few months after World War II ended, and the 1946 line was its first new one since 1942. The 46-1226 sat near the top of it. Philco's cheapest set that year, the Transitone 46-200, cost $19.70, about a tenth as much. Among the year's radio-phonographs, it ranked like this:

| Model | What it was | Tubes | Price | Today |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 46-1201 | The "Bing Crosby Special," lid-loading | 5 | $59.95 | ~$1,030 |
| 46-1203 | Table set with record changer | 6 | $114.90 | ~$1,980 |

| 46-1209 | Radio-phonograph, AM and shortwave | 8 | $205.00 | ~$3,540 |
| 46-1213 | Flagship console with FM and pushbuttons | 11 | Not known | — |

Only the 46-1209 cost more among the models with a known price. The 46-1213, with FM and pushbutton band selection in a walnut or mahogany cabinet, was the top of the line.

## What it hears

Once the radio was working, I started logging every station I could identify. AM signals travel much farther after dark, and a big console with a good antenna can pull in clear-channel stations from hundreds of miles away.

### AM, then and now

The AM dial in 1946 looked a lot like today's. Most stations had moved to their current frequencies in a single night in March 1941, when a treaty among the United States, Canada, Mexico and Cuba reshuffled the band. So when this radio was new, WLW was already at 700 and WSM at 650, right where I found them.

What's changed is who shares those frequencies after dark. In 1946, about two dozen of the most powerful stations, the "clear channels," had their frequencies almost to themselves at night, so a farm family hundreds of miles away could hear them cleanly. The FCC began letting other stations share those channels in 1961 and finished the job in 1980. Today the big signals still come in, but they have more company and more fading.

The top of the dial is marked "POLICE." In the 1930s, police departments broadcast their calls just above the AM band, where many home radios could pick them up. In 1946 there were no broadcast stations up there at all. That stretch, 1610 to 1700 kHz, wasn't opened to AM stations until the late 1990s. So CHHA in Toronto, on 1610, is a station this radio could not have received when it was new.

AM itself is fading now. WBT in Charlotte, a 50,000-watt giant, now plays a music loop telling listeners to switch to its FM station. Several others in my log, like WKIX and WTSB, also broadcast on FM.

### Shortwave, then and now

The shortwave dial glass is printed with London, Berlin, Rome, Spain, Japan and Australia. In 1946, shortwave was how Americans heard the rest of the world directly, and the bands this radio covers, 9.3 to 15.5 MHz, were where the big international broadcasters lived.

Most of them have since left. The BBC World Service, for one, stopped broadcasting to North America on shortwave in 2001. Both of my shortwave catches are religious broadcasters in Tennessee.
